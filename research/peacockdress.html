I have reseached this particular garment as a kind of case study, to explore the complex questions that can come up when historical recreations and replicas are made of potentially offensive and damaging items. The Peacock Dress was worn by Lady Mary Curzon to the Delhi Durbar Ball in 1903. Lady Curzon was married to the Lord Curzon (Viceroy of India, 1899-1905) and the Ball was thrown to entertain and impress the world with England's colonial wealth and power. The dress itself became a sensation and made world news, cementing Lady Curzon as a leader in fashion and style. It is a beautiful garment, covered in hand embroidered gold and silver peacock feathers and made of fabric traditionally worn by Mughal court rulers.

The peacock feather is an important Hindu symbol which is associated with Lord Krishna and the goddess Saraswati, and the embroidery was created by the intricate hand-stitched hook-work embroidery technique, zardozi. The fabric was made and embroidered by local crafts people in Delhi, using metal thread and green beetle wing cases, then sent to Paris where it was turned into a gown by the nortious House of Worth.

The thing that first brought this dress to my attention was when the historical costumer Cathy Hay decided to recreate the piecce so that she could wear it herself. Hay is a white British woman, who was publicly attempting to reproduce and wear a symbol of British colonialism and power over India. The Delhi Durbar Ball was an event celebrating the coronation of a British monarch in India, and an opportunity for Britain to flaunt the wealth that they gained by exploiting and ruling over the Indian people.

Hay wanting to copy this dress was very controversial and eventually she stopped sharing her work on the garment and has since removed any and all reference to the dress from her public channels. Obviously, I think that the desire to recreate this dress is pretty strange, and I am glad that Hay stopped the project, or at least stopped sharing it, and profitting from it. However, I do think that her misstep serves as an interesting reminder and challenge for people, like me, who enjoy working on historical fashion and reproductions. It is absolutely neccessary to understand the history of a style, pattern of garment before you use and wear it.
